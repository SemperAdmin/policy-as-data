Ontology

An ontology is a precise statement of what kinds of things exist in a subject and how they relate, written so a machine can check it. This page says what this project already has, what the leading formal method would add, and why it has not been adopted yet.

What this project already has

{{n_ontology_classes}}

classes in the authority ontology: statute, DoD instruction, Navy instruction, Marine Corps order, manual, message, and the relationships that rank them. The authority chains are drawn from it.

{{n_concepts}}

rule concepts, each assigned by a person who read both paragraphs. A concept is what makes "12 weeks" in a message and "84 days" in an instruction the same entitlement rather than two similar numbers.

{{edges}}

cited links, each naming the paragraph it was read from. A link the text states and a link a tool worked out are never recorded as the same kind of thing.

This is a small ontology on purpose. It describes documents and their authority. It does not yet describe the world the rules are about: who counts as a reservist, what a qualifying event is, which duty status a rule applies to. Today a person answers those questions and the answer is written down with their name on it.

The method we evaluated

The Suggested Upper Merged Ontology (SUMO), with its Sigma toolset, is described by its publisher as the largest public formal ontology. It is led by Adam Pease at the Naval Postgraduate School. It was read in full from the group's published papers in September 2026. It has four parts.

PartWhat it doesWhat the papers show
A world modelAbout 20,000 defined terms and over 100,000 logical statements, mapped by hand to the English dictionary WordNet.Mature. It works as a shared vocabulary. Its legal vocabulary is a sketch of about 500 lines.
A theorem proverTranslates the ontology into first-order logic and asks a prover whether a conclusion follows.Works at full size only with a relevance filter in front of it, which takes it to about 2 seconds a question. Content beyond first-order logic is dropped in translation, by the authors' own statement. The ontology cannot be proven free of contradictions.
Obligation and permissionMarks a statement as required, allowed, or forbidden, and says who holds the duty.Experimental. Tested on small hand-written problems. Known wrong answers in one class of case, still under investigation.
English to logicA trained model translates a sentence into the ontology's logic.On 100 real policy sentences, 59 to 62 percent of outputs were well formed. Whether they meant the same thing as the sentence was not measured.

The group's most successful rule system encodes the maritime collision rules. It was written by hand, uses plain logic rather than the obligation operators. Across 288 generated encounters it reached a conclusion in 172 and was right in 171.

How it would apply here

This projectWhat SUMO would changeFit today
The rule concept registerConcepts would point at defined terms, and a rule could cover a class of people or duties rather than one number.The strongest case. Not needed until a rule's value is a class, not a number. Promotion eligibility is the first place that happens.
The evaluatorA prover would derive the answer and show every step.Poor. No paper computes an entitlement such as a number of days, and a prover can run out of time, which is not an answer.
Checking orders against each otherA prover could find two orders that cannot both be followed.A real future need, once two or more orders with interacting rules are encoded.
Reading rules out of textA model would draft the rules.At best a drafting aid. Every drafted rule would still need a person to read the paragraph, which is what verification already requires.

What it would give

  • A model of the world the rules are about. No other candidate the project has reviewed has one.
  • Answers that show their work. A proof runs from the facts through each rule to the conclusion, which is deeper than the one citation per line this site shows today.
  • Proven date arithmetic. The group's calendar work was checked by two independent provers on 200 cases. This project has already found one error of exactly that kind, a year counted as 365 days.
  • Discipline about absence. The papers insist that failing to prove something is allowed never proves it is forbidden. This project holds the same rule.

What it would cost

  • A heavy dependency. The standard toolset needs Java, at least 16 GB of memory, and a separate prover. A lighter rewrite exists but is marked as a development build. This project builds from a plain copy with standard Python and nothing else, and that is a fixed requirement.
  • Unclear terms of use. The ontology's own site and its code repository state different licences, and no paper settles it.
  • Immature where policy needs it most. Obligations, exceptions, which rule wins when two conflict, and the dates a rule is in force are the least developed parts. A legal rules standard, LegalRuleML, covers all four.
  • One source. Every paper traces to one research group. No independent assessment has been read yet.
  • It does not address the current problem. What this site finds today is documents citing instructions that are no longer in force. That is a question of provenance, not of reasoning.

The decision

Not adopted yet, and kept under evaluation. Three of its ideas are planned for this project's own tools, without the dependency. None is built yet. They are: date arithmetic tested against fixed cases in the shape of the calendar benchmark; every computed answer printing its steps; and an explicit record when a person has read an authority and found it silent, so silence is never inferred from nothing having been read.

The question is reopened when the first rule's value is a class rather than a number, or when two encoded orders interact. At that point SUMO and LegalRuleML are tried on one worked example and the choice is made once.

Sources: the group's papers as published at adampease.com, among them SigmaKEE-rs, Embedding Deontic Logic Operators, Gregorian date arithmetic, Rules of the Road, and Grounding Terms from an Ontology. The full review, with the paper each claim was read from, is in the repository under resources/28.
